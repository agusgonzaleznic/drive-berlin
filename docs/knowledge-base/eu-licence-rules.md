# EU/EEA licence rules and the Italian angle

*Research date: 3 August 2026. Adversarial verification pass: 4 August 2026 (18 of 26 claims confirmed, 7 corrected, 3 sub-claims unverifiable). Every correction from that pass has been applied below; anything the verifier could not confirm against a primary source is marked "⚠️ unverified".*

## Bottom line

- *§ 28 FeV* (the *Fahrerlaubnis-Verordnung*, Germany's Driving Licence Regulation) turns on the **issuing state of your licence**, not on your nationality. It recognises "*Inhaber einer gültigen EU- oder EWR-Fahrerlaubnis*" — holders of a valid EU/EEA *driving entitlement*. An Argentine licence is a third-country licence, so § 28 gives you nothing, no matter how many EU passports you hold.
- Your Italian citizenship is genuinely useful, but only on the **administration** side: no residence permit exists or is needed, your Italian passport or *carta d'identità* is an accepted ID document at the *Bürgeramt* and at LABO, and your *Anmeldung* satisfies the 185-day ordinary-residence test. It does not shorten the exam requirements by a single question.
- The 185-day *ordentlicher Wohnsitz* (ordinary residence) rule in *§ 7 Abs. 1 FeV* is a prerequisite you meet as a registered Berlin resident. Watch one trap: *§ 7 Abs. 3 FeV* says EU/EEA nationals in Germany **only** to attend a university or school expressly do **not** establish ordinary residence — this is the opposite of what is often reported.
- The 4th EU Driving Licence Directive — Directive (EU) 2025/2205 of **22 October 2025**, in force **25 November 2025** — does **not** leave everything unchanged until 2029. It has a staggered timetable: 26 November 2027, 26 November 2028 and 26 November 2029 (*Art. 29*). None of the stages changes what you must do today, but two of them will change the German class B landscape before the decade is out.
- One thing in the new directive is a **tightening**, not a relaxation: *Art. 11(1) and (2)* make a medical examination the default before first issue **and** before renewal, with a self-assessment only as a Member State substitute for class B (*Art. 11(3)*). Germany currently requires neither for class B.
- If you have **ever** held an Italian *patente* — even one that expired years ago — stop and re-read the § 28 section. It changes your whole route, and *Art. 7(5)* of Directive 2006/126/EC obliges you to declare it honestly.

## 1. What *§ 28 FeV* actually says

*§ 28 Abs. 1 Satz 1 FeV* is the recognition rule for EU and EEA licences:

> Inhaber einer gültigen EU- oder EWR-Fahrerlaubnis, die ihren ordentlichen Wohnsitz im Sinne des § 7 Absatz 1 oder 2 in der Bundesrepublik Deutschland haben, dürfen – vorbehaltlich der Einschränkungen nach den Absätzen 2 bis 4 – im Umfang ihrer Berechtigung Kraftfahrzeuge im Inland führen.

*Holders of a valid EU or EEA driving entitlement who have their ordinary residence in Germany within the meaning of § 7(1) or (2) may — subject to the restrictions in paragraphs 2 to 4 — drive motor vehicles in Germany to the extent of their entitlement.* Sentence 2 adds that any conditions (*Auflagen*) endorsed on the foreign licence must also be observed in Germany. Source: [§ 28 FeV](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/fev_2010/__28.html), implementing the mutual-recognition rule in *Art. 2(1)* of [Directive 2006/126/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A32006L0126) ("Driving licences issued by Member States shall be mutually recognised").

Three points that are routinely missed:

1. **The residence cross-reference is narrow.** § 28 Abs. 1 recognises the licence of a holder with ordinary residence under *§ 7 Abs. 1 or 2* — **not** Abs. 3. Someone in Germany solely as a student under § 7 Abs. 3 therefore falls outside § 28's recognition rule as well as outside the ordinary-residence route ([§ 7 FeV](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/fev_2010/__7.html)).
2. **The scope of the entitlement is not unlimited.** *§ 28 Abs. 2 FeV* makes the German scope of an EU licence depend on Commission Decision (EU) 2016/1945 on equivalences between licence categories, and excludes categories for which that Decision shows no German equivalent. So "recognised" does not automatically mean "everything printed on the card".
3. **Moving does not require exchanging.** The *Bundesministerium für Verkehr* (BMV, Federal Ministry of Transport) confirms an EU licence "will normally remain valid … until the end of its period of validity" ([BMV, validity of foreign driving licences](https://www.bmv.de/SharedDocs/EN/Articles/StV/Roadtraffic/validity-foreign-driving-licences-in-germany.html)).

### When recognition is refused — *§ 28 Abs. 4 FeV*

Recognition is denied to holders who possess only a learner or provisionally issued permit (Nr. 1 — this is what kills the Italian *foglio rosa*); who had their ordinary residence in Germany at the time the foreign licence was issued, the *Wohnsitzverstoß*, subject to a school/university exception (Nr. 2); whose licence has been withdrawn or suspended in Germany, who are under a court-ordered ban or a current *Fahrverbot* (Nr. 3, 4); who held a German licence when they acquired the foreign one; and licences obtained on the strength of fraudulent third-country documents. Source: [§ 28 FeV](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/fev_2010/__28.html).

Nr. 2 is worth remembering as a closed door: obtaining an EU licence somewhere else while ordinarily resident in Berlin does not produce a licence Germany will recognise.

Do not confuse the €37.50 with **€26.50**, which belongs to a different service: 124556, *Umtausch eines alten Führerscheins in einen EU-Kartenführerschein* (exchanging an old German paper licence for an EU card), plus €6.32 *Direktversand* and €9.52 *Expressherstellung* — the latter only when applied for at the *Fahrerlaubnisbehörde*.

**Where the old licence goes.** You keep the EU card during processing and surrender it when collecting the German one. *Art. 11(1)* third sentence of Directive 2006/126/EC requires the exchanging state to return it to the issuing state; from 26 November 2029 the rule sits in *Art. 13(3)* of Directive (EU) 2025/2205: "The Member State exchanging a physical driving licence shall return the old driving licence to the authorities of the Member State of issuance, and give the reasons for the exchange." For mobile (digital) licences the issuing state is only notified and must disable the licence. You cannot hold both.

**A live gap worth knowing.** Your Europe warns that "some EU countries do not recognise provisional or temporary documents issued during the renewal process" — a real risk for anyone mid-conversion in Berlin who then drives to Italy. *Art. 14* of Directive (EU) 2025/2205 fixes this from 26 November 2029 by obliging the exchanging state to record the pending exchange in the EU driving licence network and to give the holder "a document indicating that a request for the replacement or exchange of that holder's driving licence has been made". Until then, the gap is open.

## 3. The 185-day ordinary-residence rule

*§ 7 Abs. 1 FeV* permits a German licence only to applicants with *ordentlicher Wohnsitz* in Germany. The EU definition it implements, *Art. 12* of Directive 2006/126/EC:

> normal residence means the place where a person usually lives, that is for at least 185 days in each calendar year, because of personal and occupational ties

**Proof in Berlin** is your *Anmeldung* and *Meldebescheinigung* (registration certificate); LABO checks the Berlin population register directly, and *Hauptwohnsitz* in Berlin is an explicit requirement on the Berlin licence services, with *Nebenwohnsitz* accepted only in justified exceptions and with the consent of the main-residence authority. As a registered permanent resident of Berlin, you satisfy this already.

**The student inversion.** It is widely written that EU/EEA students attending a German institution for six months or more "also qualify" via ordinary residence. That is backwards. *§ 7 Abs. 3 Satz 1 FeV* says such nationals, staying in Germany exclusively to attend a *Hochschule* or *Schule*, "*begründen keinen ordentlichen Wohnsitz im Inland*" — they do **not** establish ordinary residence. They may be issued a licence under a **separate** exception when the stay lasts at least six months. The distinction is not academic: § 28 Abs. 1 recognises EU licences only of holders resident under § 7 Abs. 1 or 2.

**Marked as changing.** From 26 November 2029, *Art. 20(1)* of Directive (EU) 2025/2205 restates the test as **185 days of the previous 365 days** rather than "185 days in each calendar year", and states expressly that "Attendance at a university or school shall not constitute transfer of normal residence." The corresponding condition for issuing a licence is *Art. 10(1)(e)*.

## 4. You, specifically: Italian passport, Argentine licence

### Why § 28 does not help you

§ 28 Abs. 1 Satz 1 attaches to the *Fahrerlaubnis* — the driving entitlement — and asks a single question about it: was it issued by an EU or EEA state? Your entitlement was issued by Argentina. Nationality appears nowhere in the provision, in *Art. 2(1)* of Directive 2006/126/EC, or in the BMV's summary of it. An Italian passport is therefore invisible to the recognition machinery. Concretely:

- You cannot drive in Germany on the Argentine licence as an EU-licence holder. Your right to drive on it rests on the entirely separate third-country regime, not on § 28.
- You cannot exchange the Argentine licence under Berlin service 121598, which requires an EU/EEA licence.
- Your route is the third-country *Umschreibung*, which sits in a different part of the FeV (§§ 29 ff. and **Anlage 11 FeV**, the annex listing states whose licences convert with reduced or no examination). Because Argentina is not listed in Anlage 11, **both** the German theory and the German practical examination are required. ⚠️ Note on sourcing: the research behind *this* file did not verify §§ 29–31 FeV or Anlage 11 — the Argentina-specific documents in this knowledge base (`raw/ar-legal-core.json`, `raw/ar-berlin-process.json` and the derived guides) are the authority for that route, and the section numbers here should be read against them rather than relied on from this page.
- The retained-but-unverified note that *§ 30 FeV* is the operative provision for issuing a German licence on the basis of a foreign one is consistent with § 28 Abs. 3 Satz 4 FeV, which cross-refers to § 30 — but it was **not** re-verified in the primary-text pass. ⚠️ unverified.

### The tempting shortcut, and why it closes

Converting the Argentine licence into an **Italian** one first, then walking it into Germany under § 28, does not work from Berlin. Issue and renewal of an EU licence are tied to normal residence in the issuing Member State (*Art. 7(3)(b)* of Directive 2006/126/EC for renewal; *Art. 10(1)(e)* of Directive (EU) 2025/2205 for issue), so Italy would need you resident in Italy. And if an EU licence were nonetheless issued while you were ordinarily resident in Germany, *§ 28 Abs. 4 Satz 1 Nr. 2 FeV* denies German recognition outright — the *Wohnsitzverstoß*. Whether Italy has a conversion agreement with Argentina at all, and on what terms, **this research does not have**; the Italian Ministry's own conversion page ([mit.gov.it](https://www.mit.gov.it/conversione-patente-estera)) is where to check it.

The practical examination, by contrast, can reportedly only be taken in German — ⚠️ **unverified**, retained from an earlier research run and not re-checked against a primary source. Assume it is true and plan for it; confirm with your *Fahrschule* and with TÜV/DEKRA.

### What it does **not** get you

No exam exemption, no fee reduction, no shortened *Fahrschule* requirement, and no credit for anything done in Argentina. Italian first-aid courses are also useless here: *§ 19 FeV* requires "die Bescheinigung einer für solche Schulungen amtlich anerkannten Stelle oder eines Trägers der öffentlichen Verwaltung" — a certificate from a body **officially recognised in Germany** — with a minimum of nine units of 45 minutes. That door is genuinely closed.

The eye test is more open than usually reported. *§ 12 FeV* prescribes "Der Sehtest wird von einer amtlich anerkannten Sehteststelle unter Einhaltung der DIN 58220 Teil 6 durchgeführt", with the certificate no more than two years old at application — **but** § 12 also allows an alternative route, a *Zeugnis* or *Gutachten* from an ophthalmologist (*Augenarzt*), and does **not** expressly require that ophthalmologist to be German-licensed. The accurate formulation: the *Sehtest* route requires a German officially recognised *Sehteststelle* and Berlin uses German-standard forms, but the FeV contains no express exclusion of an EU ophthalmologist's report. The earlier claim that "Italian eye tests are not accepted" is overstated.

### If you have ever held an Italian *patente*

Two consequences, and they matter more than anything else on this page.

1. **Declare it.** *Art. 7(5)* of Directive 2006/126/EC: "(a) No person may hold more than one driving licence; (b) A Member State shall refuse to issue a licence where it establishes that the applicant already holds a driving licence". Note the nuance in (c): verification with other Member States is triggered where "there are reasonable grounds to suspect" a second licence — it is **not** run automatically on every applicant. That makes the honest declaration your responsibility, not a formality the system will catch for you.
2. **Your route changes.** An Italian licence in hand, even expired, puts you on the § 28 / service-121598 track described in section 2 — €37.50 and no exams — instead of the two-exam Argentine route. If the Italian card is expired but in your possession, service.berlin.de 121598 accepts it as-is. If you are **not in possession** of it (theft or loss), the Italian embassy page indicates an *attestato di validità* from the Motorizzazione is needed, obtainable online via the *portale dell'automobilista*. Two warnings: the trigger is not-in-possession, **not** mere expiry, and neither the reported "~€42" cost nor the reported sworn-translation (*traduttore giurato*) requirement appears on any official source — ⚠️ both **unverified**. Ask the *Bürgeramt* or LABO before paying for either.

The EU licence **card itself never needs translation** for use or exchange in Germany: service 121598 lists it with no translation requirement and no FeV provision imposes one. For supporting Italian documents, treat a certified German translation as something an office *may* ask for and confirm case-by-case, not as a published rule.

The Italian Embassy in Berlin directs residents of Germany "*esclusivamente alle Autorità competenti per il luogo di residenza*" — exclusively to the authority for their place of residence — and names *Landesamt für Bürger- und Ordnungsangelegenheiten, Puttkamerstraße 16–18, Tel. (030) 90269-2300*. It also advises that it is "*consigliato convertire la patente di guida italiana*". The embassy page gives the postcode as **10958 Berlin** while Berlin's own service pages give **10969 Berlin** for the same address; the Berlin authority's own listing is the more authoritative of the two.

Two claims often attached to this — that consular licence renewal exists only in non-EU countries, and that *rinnovo* of an Italian licence is possible only in Italy at the Motorizzazione Civile — appear on **neither** cited source. ⚠️ Both **unverified**; do not rely on them.

Separately and unrelated to driving: registration with **AIRE** (*Anagrafe degli Italiani Residenti all'Estero*) is compulsory for Italians resident abroad under Legge 27 ottobre 1988, n. 470, and the declaration of transfer of residence must be made to the competent consular office **within 90 days** of the move. It has no effect on German licensing; it governs your access to consular services.

## 5. A rule aimed at a different persona, but worth flagging

*§ 2a Abs. 1 StVG* extends the German two-year probationary period (*Probezeit*) to incoming EU licence holders: "*Die Regelungen über die Fahrerlaubnis auf Probe finden auch Anwendung auf Inhaber einer gültigen Fahrerlaubnis aus einem Mitgliedstaat der Europäischen Union … die ihren ordentlichen Wohnsitz in das Inland verlegt haben*", with time already held credited — "*Die Zeit seit dem Erwerb der Fahrerlaubnis ist auf die Probezeit anzurechnen*" ([§ 2a StVG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stvg/__2a.html)).

**Who this applies to:** an Italian who passed the *patente B* less than two years before moving to Berlin. They are inside the remainder of the German *Probezeit* — *Aufbauseminar* and point consequences included — **even without exchanging the licence**. It does not describe your Argentine-licence route; how *Probezeit* attaches on a third-country *Umschreibung* is a question for the Argentina-specific documents in this knowledge base.

## 6. The 4th EU Driving Licence Directive

### Identity and status

Directive (EU) 2025/2205 of the European Parliament and of the Council of **22 October 2025** on driving licences. Signature block: "Done at Strasbourg, 22 October 2025" — the frequently seen "22/23 October" is wrong. Published OJ L, 2025/2205, **5 November 2025**, alongside Directive (EU) 2025/2206 on the EU-wide effect of driving disqualifications. *Art. 31*: "This Directive shall enter into force on the twentieth day following that of its publication in the Official Journal of the European Union" — i.e. **25 November 2025**, which the European Commission confirmed in its news item of that date. The European Parliament plenary voted on 21 October 2025. German Wikipedia gives 26 November 2025 as the entry into force; that is **wrong** and should not be used.

### The timetable is staggered — *Art. 29*

| Later, date not yet fixed | Mobile (digital) licence becomes the **default** issuing format, 54 months after the first implementing act under *Art. 5(7)*; mutual recognition of mobile licences only from that date | Art. 3(4), Art. 3(6) |

Verbatim, *Art. 29*:

> 1. Without prejudice to paragraph 2, by 26 November 2028, Member States shall adopt and publish the measures necessary to comply with this Directive. … they shall apply those measures from 26 November 2029. 2. By 26 November 2027, Member States shall adopt and publish the measures necessary to comply with Article 9(2), points (j) and (k). … They shall apply those measures from 26 November 2027. 3. By 26 November 2028, Member States shall adopt and publish the measures necessary to comply with Article 17. … They shall apply those measures from 26 November 2028.

**Two corrections to widely circulated summaries.** First, "nothing changes before end-2029" is wrong — the 2027 and 2028 stages are real. Second, an earlier verification run at this path claimed *Art. 29(2)* concerns licence **validity periods**; the OJ text is explicit that it concerns *Art. 9(2)(j)* and *(k)*, the vehicle equivalences. Validity periods (*Art. 10(2)*) follow the general 2028/2029 timetable. The Commission's own shorthand: general rules in four years, alternatively powered vehicles in two, accompanied driving in three.

The "digital licence around 2030" figure that circulates widely is a guess. The actual trigger is *Art. 3(4)*: 54 months from the adoption of the first implementing act under *Art. 5(7)*. A mobile licence issued **before** that date — permitted by *Art. 3(5)* — is **not** recognised cross-border. Holders always retain the right to a physical card (*Art. 3(4)*, *Art. 4(1)*).

### What this means for Germany, and for you

Already German law, independent of the new directive:

- Two-year *Probezeit*: *§ 2a Abs. 1 StVG* — "die Probezeit dauert zwei Jahre vom Zeitpunkt der Erteilung an". Verified.
- 15-year card validity: *§ 24a FeV* — "Die Gültigkeit der ab dem 19. Januar 2013 ausgestellten Führerscheine ist auf 15 Jahre befristet". Verified.
- Accompanied driving at 17 (*BF17*), reportedly *§ 48a FeV* since 2011, currently valid inside Germany only. ⚠️ The section number and year were **not** re-verified. A retained earlier claim that BF17 is additionally recognised in Austria (reciprocal with Austria's *L17*) is likewise ⚠️ **unverified** — check [ADAC](https://www.adac.de/verkehr/rund-um-den-fuehrerschein/erwerb/begleitetes-fahren/) or an Austrian source before relying on it.

Changes Germany must make, with dates:

- **From 26 November 2027:** the 4.25 t alternatively-fuelled-vehicle equivalence — the earliest German change of all.
- **From 26 November 2028:** EU-wide validity of accompanied driving at 17. Note this is 2028, **not** 2029.
- **By 26 November 2029:** Germany must introduce either a medical examination or one of the *Art. 11(3)* alternatives for class B, both at first issue and at renewal. Today Germany requires neither. Frame this as a **tightening** of the German class B position, not as a new optional convenience.

A retained, unverified note holds that Germany has adopted legislation for a **national** digital driving licence targeted for the end of 2026, ahead of the EU rollout. ⚠️ **Unverified**; treat as a lead, not a fact.

**For your own timeline:** none of these dates changes what you do now. Your application, your two exams and your fees are governed by the current FeV and by Directive 2006/126/EC, which remains in force until 26 November 2029. What the directive does affect is what happens to your German licence **later**: your first card will carry a 15-year validity, and its renewal will fall under the new *Art. 11(2)* fitness regime.

## 7. Looking further ahead: a German licence, then Italy

A German licence is fully valid in Italy — *Art. 2(1)* of Directive 2006/126/EC, and MIT's own wording, "*Le patenti di guida rilasciate da stati appartenenti all'Unione Europea o allo Spazio Economico Europeo sono equiparate alle patenti italiane*". No notification to the Motorizzazione or to the consulate is required when you obtain a German licence; where a conversion is involved, Germany itself returns the old licence to the issuing state (*Art. 11(1)* → *Art. 13(3)* of 2025/2205), and the registers communicate through the EU driving licence network (*Art. 7(5)(d)*, *Art. 15*). Note that "no notification needed" is an inference from the absence of a requirement rather than an express MIT statement.

**If you later move back to Italy — a widely repeated error corrected.** The rule allowing a host state to impose its own administrative validity periods "as from 2 years after the date on which the holder has taken up normal residence on its territory" is *Art. 2(2)* of Directive 2006/126/EC (**not** Art. 7(3)), and its successor from 26 November 2029 is *Art. 4(3)* of Directive (EU) 2025/2205 in identical terms. Critically, it applies **only** to a licence "**WITHOUT** the administrative validity period set out in Article 7(2)". A German class B card issued today has a 15-year administrative validity under *§ 24a FeV* and is therefore **not** caught: it stays valid to its printed expiry and is only then renewed or converted in Italy. Do **not** believe the common claim that Italy can impose a medical on a modern German card after two years of Italian residence. MIT's own framing matches: holders of EU/EEA licences lacking an expiry date, or with validity exceeding EU standards, must convert after two years of normal residence.

Your Europe's caveat still applies to whatever you renew there: a renewed licence "will be subject to any restrictions or conditions that apply in your new country."

Two related points to treat with care:

- Italian class B renewal intervals, and the claim that Italy requires a medical at every ten-year renewal with shorter intervals from age 50, could **not** be verified against an official Italian source (mit.gov.it renewal page returned 404, aci.it redirected, esteri.it was bot-protected). ⚠️ **Unverified.**
- A retained note holds that an EU licence expired by **more** than five years requires a *revisione* (test/fitness procedure) before Italy reissues, while one expired by less than five years needs only the medical. ⚠️ **Unverified.**
