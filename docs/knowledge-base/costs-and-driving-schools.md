# Costs and choosing a Berlin driving school

*Scope: a holder of an Argentine car licence, resident in Berlin, with limited German. The route assumed throughout is the* Umschreibung *(conversion) of a non-EU licence under § 31 FeV, which for Argentina requires both German exams.*

**Source status.** Two research files underlie this document. The costs and schools research (`costs-schools.json`) has been through an adversarial verification pass dated 4 August 2026, and where the verifier corrected the researcher, the corrected value is used here and the researcher's original is discarded. The Berlin conversion-procedure research (`ar-berlin-process.json`, also dated 4 August 2026) has **no verification file**, so every figure that comes only from it is marked "⚠️ unverified" below. All prices are as at early August 2026.

## Bottom line

- **Your route is cheap by German standards, because the expensive part is exempt.** § 31 Abs. 2 FeV disapplies *die Vorschriften über die Ausbildung* (the rules on formal training) for licences from states not listed in Anlage 11 FeV. You owe no mandatory theory course and none of the 12 *Sonderfahrten* (special drives) — which alone would cost roughly €700–920 at observed Berlin rates. You pay fees, documents, and only as many refresher lessons as you actually need.
- **The unavoidable official fees come to €199.92**: authority conversion fee €45.10 + theory exam €24.99 + practical exam €129.83. Add documents (first aid, translation, eye test, photo) and you are at roughly €325–340 before a driving school quotes you anything.
- **Realistically budget €850–1,700 all-in.** That band is my arithmetic from individually sourced unit prices, not a figure any source publishes. The conversion research's own estimate is €700–1,400 (⚠️ unverified, and its low end assumes both few lessons and the cheaper first-aid provider).
- **A full first licence, for comparison, costs €2,200–4,500** depending on source and school, with the industry average at €3,228 for 2025. You are avoiding most of that.
- **Almost no Berlin school publishes a conversion package price — you must ask.** The verification pass could not find a single published, fixed *Umschreibung* package at any Berlin school; every school either quotes on request or publishes per-item prices only. Get the presentation fee per exam and the lesson price in writing before you enrol.
- **Do not use drivingschoolberlin.de.** Its advertised prices are far below the Berlin market and its listed phone numbers carry French (+33) and Belgian (+32) country codes rather than German ones. See the warning section.
- **One genuinely useful language finding:** the theory exam is available in Italian and Spanish as well as English, and the English translations are widely criticised. Sitting it in Spanish or Italian is probably your best option.

## Why the conversion route is structured differently

It is worth being precise about what you are and are not buying, because it changes which parts of every published Berlin price list are relevant to you.

Argentina is not listed in Anlage 11 FeV, the annex naming states with which Germany has agreed simplified conversion. Under § 31 Abs. 2 FeV, for a licence from an unlisted state only the training provisions fall away. Everything else in § 31 Abs. 1 FeV continues to apply, which means § 15 FeV (*Befähigungsprüfung* — the competence examination, both theory and practical), § 12 Abs. 2 FeV (*Sehtest*, eye test) and § 19 FeV (first aid) are all mandatory for you.

The practical consequence for your budget: you need a driving school for three things only, and none of them is a course.

| What you need a school for | Why |
| --- | --- |
| To be named on the application | The *Prüfauftrag* (examination order) is issued to the school and the *Prüfstelle* (test centre) named on your form, so both must be chosen **before** the *Bürgeramt* appointment. ⚠️ unverified |
| To present you to both exams | You cannot book with TÜV or DEKRA yourself; the school books against the *Prüfauftrag*. ⚠️ unverified |
| Refresher lessons | No legal minimum, no maximum. Entirely your judgement. |

There is no legally required lesson count on this route. Asphalt Profis, the one Berlin school with a dedicated non-EU conversion page, puts the honest version of this well: there is no minimum, but "without preparation you will not be able to pass a driving test."

### B197, and a caveat for later

B197 (available since 1 April 2021) lets you train and take the practical exam on an automatic while still being licensed for manual cars: you take at least ten 45-minute lessons in a manual, plus a test drive of at least 15 minutes with the instructor, and the resulting licence carries code 197 with no restriction anywhere in the EU. That is unlike code 78, which is a genuine automatic-only restriction. At PRIMA the B197 block costs about €50 more than the same ten units bought individually, so "roughly the same price, lower failure risk" is a fair summary. **The caveat the original research omitted:** ADAC warns that a code-197 holder who later adds class C1, C or D receives a code 78 restriction on those classes unless that higher-class exam is taken on a manual vehicle.

## How to choose a school, and what to compare

Two structural points first. Schools in Germany are under a *Preisaushang* duty — they must publicly display and honour their price lists under the *Fahrlehrergesetz* (FahrlG), confirmed by ADAC. So asking for the list is normal and you should expect to receive it. And because lesson volume dominates every bill, comparing *Grundbetrag* alone is actively misleading: AS Fahrschule has a 75% higher basic fee **and** a 28% higher lesson price than PRIMA, so the gap compounds rather than offsetting.

For your route specifically, ask these in order:

1. **Do you handle a *Drittstaat* (third-country) *Umschreibung*, and have you done Argentine licences before?** Only one Berlin school found — Asphalt Profis — documents the procedure publicly.
2. **Which *Prüfstelle* do you use, TÜV or DEKRA, and what is its exact address?** You need the school's name and address plus the test centre's on your application form so the *Prüfauftrag* can be issued and sent there. Turning up at the *Bürgeramt* without them risks a delay. ⚠️ unverified
3. **What is the presentation fee for the theory exam and for the practical exam, separately, and is it charged again on a retake?** Expect €180–220 for the practical in Berlin. Since you have no *Grundbetrag* for a theory presentation fee to hide inside, get that one confirmed explicitly.
4. **Is there a registration or admin fee for someone not taking the theory course?** The research does not have a figure for this.
5. **What is the lesson price per 45 minutes, and are longer units charged at the same per-minute rate?**
6. **What language will my practical lessons be in?** Your instructor needs to communicate with you in a language you actually think in. Spanish or English, not German.
7. **How many lessons per week can you actually give me?** Berlin has an instructor shortage; ask for two or more per week and get a straight answer.
8. **How long is the current practical-exam slot wait through you?** Independently reported at up to 8 weeks in Berlin (commonly 6–8) as at April 2026.
9. Automatic and B197 fleet availability, if that matters to you. One caution: pure automatic training yields an automatic-only licence unless B197 is arranged deliberately.

Two forward-looking notes. Official price and pass-rate data for every school is to be published on the *Mobilithek* portal — but that is a **reform element, not currently available**, and the reform is not in force. And the reform's headline savings (abolishing compulsory in-person theory, abolishing the fixed 12 *Sonderfahrten*) are **largely irrelevant to your route**, because § 31 Abs. 2 FeV already exempts you from the training rules. What would help you is the planned shorter practical exam and the price transparency. Reform status is covered below.

## Unresolved conflicts in the source material

I could not settle these from the research available, and you should verify each at the counter or with the provider.

**1. The first-aid course price: €59.99 or €72.99?** The procedure research names *Erste Hilfe Station*, Frankfurter Allee 96 in Friedrichshain, at **€59.99** in English, including a free eye test and free biometric photos. The verification pass, re-fetching prices adversarially on 4 August 2026, gives **€72.99 booked online / €82.99 paid on site** for first-aid-course-english-berlin.com — also including a free eye test and free biometric photos. These may be two providers or one business with a stale price quoted in the unverified file. The verified figure is more authoritative because it was re-fetched against the provider's own price page; the €59.99 sits in the file with no verification. **Budget €73–83 and confirm.** Both sources agree on the valuable part: the bundle covers three application requirements in one appointment.

**2. The dispatch fee: €5.31 or €6.32?** The *Ersterteilung* page (service 121627) lists **€6.32** for optional registered dispatch, and that figure was verified verbatim. The LABO fee notice lists **€5.31** for *FSW Direktversand*, cross-checked by berlinecho.de but sitting in the unverified file. They attach to different service pages and may simply be different products (registered mail versus standard direct dispatch). Neither source is wrong on its own page. Ask which applies to service 327537.

**3. Practical exam duration: 45 or 55 minutes?** The procedure research says approximately 45 minutes for class B. The verification pass checked this against statute: **FeV Anlage 7 No. 2.3 prescribes 55 minutes total with a minimum of 30 minutes of actual driving**, extended from 45 minutes with effect from 1 January 2021. **The statutory reading wins** — the procedure file appears to be quoting the pre-2021 duration. An exam taken solely to lift an automatic restriction or remove code 197 is 10 minutes shorter. This matters to your budget only indirectly: the 2021 extension is the root cause of the examiner-throughput squeeze behind Berlin's exam waits.

## Things that will save or cost you money

**Sit the theory exam in Spanish or Italian.** The exam is available in German plus twelve foreign languages — English, French, Greek, **Italian**, Polish, Portuguese, Romanian, Russian, Croatian, **Spanish**, Turkish and standard Arabic — with the practical exam in German only. The verification pass called this the biggest miss in the original research: Italian is an official exam language, and given the widely reported poor quality of the English translations, Spanish or Italian is likely a better choice for you than English. Interpreters are **not** permitted; an exam in a foreign language is possible only if that language is listed in FeV Anlage 7.

**Study from a current question catalogue.** The class B catalogue was cut from 1,197 to 1,040 questions on **1 April 2025**, and that update introduced a new question format — *Abbildungsauswahlaufgaben* (image-selection tasks allowing multiple correct answers) — plus content on e-scooters, cargo bikes and revised THC thresholds. The catalogue updates every 1 April and 1 October. Anyone studying from a pre-April-2025 app or book is working from a materially different exam. This matters more for passing first time than any reform does.

**Know what failure actually costs, from official numbers.** The TÜV|DEKRA *arge tp 21* statistics for class B/BF17 give **44% theory failure and 37% practical failure for 2025** (45% and 37% for 2024). Across all classes in 2025: 41% theory, 31% practical. For **first attempts specifically**, theory failure was 38% in 2024, against **56% on retakes** — which is the strongest available argument for not walking in underprepared. About 39% of all class B theory exams and 31% of practical exams are retakes.

> ⚠️ **Correction you should know about:** the figures "53% fail theory / 44% fail practical **in Berlin**" appear in circulating guides and were in the original research. They are **not verified Berlin-specific first-attempt rates**. No statistics body publishes a *Bundesland* breakdown in these releases, and the 53% first-attempt theory figure directly contradicts the official 38%. Use the national class B figures above and note plainly that Berlin is *widely reported* as worse than average without an official number behind it.

**An Italian first-aid certificate will not work.** Foreign and online first-aid certificates are **not accepted** for a German licence. The 9 × 45-minute (~7.5 hour) course must be taken in person in Germany. This is a common and costly assumption among EU applicants.

**Sequence the first-aid appointment first.** Because the English course bundles the eye test and the biometric photos, booking it before anything else clears three checklist items and removes roughly €16–17 of separate side costs.

**Berlin brings tighter identity checks.** Berlin is the only *Bundesland* singled out in the official 2025 exam statistics, and for cheating rather than difficulty: 497 registered fraud cases, the steepest year-on-year rise nationally. Bring valid photo ID to the test centre.

**Do not buy a car early.** The LABO's third-country conversion queue was at roughly **7 weeks** as at 28 July 2026 — the slowest conversion queue it runs, against about 5 days for *Anlage 11* states and 4 weeks for EU licences (⚠️ unverified, though the underlying LABO page is official). Practical-exam slot waits in Berlin are independently reported at up to 8 weeks. Combined with the six-month limit in § 29 Abs. 1 FeV on driving on your Argentine licence, you will very likely face a period in which you may not drive at all except on instructional drives in a school car. That is a scheduling fact with a financial edge: money spent on a vehicle before the card arrives is money parked.

**Watch the live processing page rather than a fixed week count.** LABO publishes *Aktuelle Bearbeitungsstände* (current processing status), which states the receipt date up to which each application type has been processed. It moves, and LABO warns explicitly that the status date "does not permit any conclusions about a possible collection date".

### Should you wait for the reform? No.

Whether the reform lowers prices at all is unproven. DVPI states plainly that the cost effect "cannot currently be reliably determined", and TÜV-Verband supports modernisation while warning against blanket cuts to the question catalogue. Fixed school costs — instructors, cars, fuel, insurance — keep rising, and digitising theory shifts cost without removing practice-lesson volume, which dominates every bill.

For you the case against waiting is even stronger, because the reform's two biggest savings levers are things you are **already exempt from**. And there is a concrete risk in waiting that has nothing to do with price: *WirtschaftsWoche* reports that the reform announcement has already caused prospective learners to postpone enrolment, straining driving schools. In a city with a ~7-week authority queue and 6–8 week exam waits, a post-July-2027 surge of deferred demand is a realistic hazard. Note also that WiWo vaguely places implementation at "the year-end transition", conflicting with the 1 July 2027 date in the official sources — **prefer the official date**.

Finally, the six-month clock in § 29 Abs. 1 FeV is already running from the date you established your Berlin *Hauptwohnsitz*. Waiting is not a neutral act.
